About Me

I grew up in Shenzhen and recently graduated from Shenzhen Senior High School's International Division, where I completed A-Levels in Mathematics, Further Mathematics, Physics, Economics, and Chinese. This autumn I'll begin studying Data Science at University College London.

I arrived at data science through basketball — I've been an NBA fan for years, and what started as downloading box-score data into Excel to settle arguments about player comparisons grew into an interest in era-adjusted metrics, pace-normalised statistics, and the limits of raw counting stats. That thread led to Python, to machine learning on edX, and eventually to a summer in Shanghai analysing muon detection rates from cosmic ray detectors — a project that became my first published paper.

Along the way I built an underwater robot with PID-based stability control, explored brain-computer interface research at a CAS laboratory, and studied data-driven engineering at the University of Hong Kong. Outside of academics, I founded a small online tutoring initiative for children from Shenzhen's urban village communities, participated in my school's Computer Science Club, and play basketball.

I listen to music systematically, keep a running log of films, read widely, follow the NBA, and experiment with AI tools — for writing, data analysis, music production, and building interactive web projects. I'm drawn to problems where the right question matters more than the right answer.

Summer 2024 — Shanghai
Cosmic Ray Detection — Statistical Analysis of Muon Flux

The project that led to my first publication. Worked with six Cosmic Watch detectors — compact scintillator-based particle detectors — collecting time-stamped event data and coincident atmospheric pressure readings. Organised raw detector logs into structured formats; developed a time-window algorithm for coincidence event identification and noise reduction; modelled muon detection rate as a function of atmospheric pressure using OLS linear regression. Found a statistically significant inverse correlation, consistent with the barometric effect.

Six detectors (silicon photomultipliers + plastic scintillators), microsecond-precision timestamps. Hourly bins normalised against live-time. Single-detector events treated as background. Linear regression of muon rate vs. pressure: negative slope, p < 0.01. Published in Theoretical and Natural Science, Vol. 107 (2025).

Exploring the Relationship Between Muon Detection Rates and Atmospheric Pressure Using Cosmic Watch Detectors
Theoretical and Natural Science, Volume 107, pp. 157–163
Published 6 May 2025 · Received 18 November 2024 · Open Access (CC BY)
ISSN (Print): 2753-8818 · ISSN (Online): 2753-8826
DOI: 10.54254/2753-8818/2025.22629
Abstract
This study explores how atmospheric pressure affects muon detection rates. By analyzing data from six Cosmic Watch detectors, we organized the collected information, examined the correlation between atmospheric pressure and muon detection rates, and addressed the timing discrepancies across detectors. Our findings show that muon detection rates decrease as atmospheric pressure rises — consistent with the barometric effect in cosmic ray physics. The study also improves time difference correction methods, providing new strategies and tools for detecting rare coincidence events recorded by multiple detectors.
